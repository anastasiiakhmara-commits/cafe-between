Autumn rain is pouring outside, drumming on the tin sill outside the town square window, and turning the thick old glass of the other window almost opaque. The innkeeper listens carefully to the distant sound of thunder as he’s wiping mismatched clay mugs queued in front of him on the bar; might be the last this year, he thinks.

A door bursts open, illuminated by a flash of lightning. Not the town door, the other one – old and arched and creaky, built without a single metal nail. A hooded man is standing on the threshold, rain glistening on his long cloak; his movements are unnaturally fast and fluid. He steps in, and a gust of wind seems to step in with him, shaking drops of water from his clothes, rustling the leaves of cafe plants, almost snatching the dried yellow leaf from its shelf.

The innkeeper snatches the leaf mid-air and restores it carefully to its place, then looks at the visitor with mild disapproval.

‘And now I have actual puddles right in front of the door,’ he says.

The visitor takes off his hood, revealing an ageless face with too sharp features, bright green eyes and unruly straw-colored hair. He laughs, turning to hang his cloak on a peg by the door.

‘As if you ever were bothered by a bit of rainwater,’ he says, shaking his head. He still closes the door, and the wind calms down, too.

The innkeeper shrugs, hiding a smile in the corners of his mouth, and chooses the biggest clay cup.

‘Coffee or tea?’ he asks.

‘Tea,’ the newcomer answers decisively, ‘with something stronger for good measure.’

The innkeeper is already reaching for an old bottle of deep amber liquid.

‘You have travelled all the way from the mountains, with the storm,’ he says, not quite a question.

The straw-haired visitor smiles a little smugly.

‘You know how the Lord of the Peaks is. If he needs something, he needs the message delivered immediately, and with as much drama as possible.’

‘And still, you are here, and not wherever you were hurrying to,’ the innkeeper observes, sliding the steaming cup towards him.

‘My… recipient won’t be up till twilight anyway, and I’d rather wait for an hour here than elsewhere,’ the messenger replies.

The innkeeper raises his brow, but the visitor waves his hand vaguely.

‘Don’t look at me like that, I’ve already told you more than I should have!’ he protests.

‘I have no need of your secrets,’ the innkeeper replies, a beat too fast to sound entirely sincere, and returns to wiping his cups.

The messenger drinks up the tea and stretches contentedly, his arms and legs seeming to actually elongate as he does so.

‘Almost forgot,’ he says, ‘I have something for you.’

He reaches into his canvas bag and produces a half-full pack of coffee. It’s slightly garish and surprisingly ordinary, held closed with a binder clip.

The innkeeper picks it up and examines it carefully.

‘And that’s from…?’ he prompts.

The visitor flashes another smug smile.

‘From twenty years ago. I’d thought you would like that.’

The innkeeper nods approvingly and opens the pack. The coffee is roasted so dark it’s almost burnt; it smells slightly bitter, but with an unmistakable note of something having just begun.

He stores the pack on a shelf and turns back to the visitor, contemplative.

‘There’s a new Lady of the Lake,’ he says. ‘You might want to pay your respects.’

And, after a pause:

‘Also, I think something has crawled into the swamps to the east of the town. Won’t be dangerous for you, of course, but it might slow you down.’

The messenger looks up from his cup.

‘I have heard about the Lady, but not about the swamps.’

He looks thoughtful for a moment, as if hesitating if anything else should be said, then asks:

‘Have you heard from the old Chmurnik from up in the hills lately?’

The innkeeper shakes his head slowly.

‘Not recently. Why?’

‘No specific reason. It’s just he is usually restless when the summer ends, always calls me for a message to this or that household that he feels isn’t acting respectful enough – but not this year, it seems.’

‘He might have accepted that some rituals are simply not being observed anymore,’ the innkeeper suggests doubtfully.

Both chuckle. Both consider it unlikely.

The messenger leaves soon after finishing his tea. The innkeeper looks out of the window at the storm clouds, lost in thought.
