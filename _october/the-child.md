The door opens a crack – the town door, the one facing the lively square – and a small, blonde curly head peeps in. The bell above chimes gently, once.

The door is too heavy for the young visitor; a boy no older than six. He has to push it with all his weight to get inside, but he manages.

‘Good morning,’ he says politely, looking around the cafe, taking in the house plants everywhere and the dried herbs the innkeeper is sorting carefully on the bar. ‘Are you making a potion?’

‘Good morning,’ the innkeeper answers, his face carefully neutral. ‘I’m making herbal tea.’ Then, after a moment of hesitation: ‘Would you like some cocoa?’

‘Can I have just milk?’ the kid answers, somewhat shyly. ‘Please,’ he adds hastily.

‘Sure. Some of my regulars have… a peculiar love for milk, so I always keep a bottle,’ the innkeeper says, keeping amusement out of his voice and reaching under the bar.

‘Like that cat?’ the kid asks curiously, squatting next to the half-empty bowl in the corner and staring into the shadows. ‘Pspsps,’ he goes, as the Thing stares at him from the dark corner; it’s hard to tell whether the Thing is alarmed or annoyed.

‘The cat is shy and doesn’t like coming out to visitors,’ the innkeeper intervenes quickly. Thankfully, the kid has already shifted his attention to the Venus flytrap growing in the corner.

‘Oh wow. Is it the plant that eats insects? I’ve seen something like that when we went to the palm house,’ the kid says, bending over the pot curiously.

‘Careful, it bites,’ the innkeeper says, stepping out of the bar in case either the boy or the plant need rescuing.

The boy takes a step back and hides his hands behind his back, his curious gaze fixing on the other door – the ancient arched one.

‘Where does this one lead?’ he asks, taking a step towards the threshold. ‘It looks so old…’

The innkeeper seems to cover the whole distance between the bar and the door in one fluid, impossibly long step, to stand between the kid and the door, the bottle of milk still in his hand.

‘No visitors allowed here,’ he says sternly. ‘Take a seat. Don’t touch anything. I’ll give you the milk.’

The kid looks up, looking somewhat offended.

‘I was just asking!’

He shuffles towards the bar and climbs on the bar stool, resting his elbows on the bar and dangling his feet in the air – the image of patience.

The innkeeper lets out a long exhale, comes back and reaches up for a smooth clay cup.

The boy stares at the shelf above the bar, full of varied jars and bottles.

‘Is all of that for herbal tea?’ he asks, hesitant. ‘Are those really bug wings? And why is that sugar black?’

‘Yes, that’s for the tea,’ the innkeeper says firmly. ‘And do you really think someone would add bug wings to the tea?’

‘You also have mushrooms over there,’ the boy points out. ‘Do you add mushrooms to tea?’

The innkeeper sighs.

‘Mushrooms are for soup.’

‘Yuck,’ the boy says. ‘Who would want soup in a cafe?’

The innkeeper leaves that one with no answer and slides the cup he’s finally filled towards the boy carefully.

‘Drink your milk.’

‘Thank you,’ the boy says politely, and hesitates a moment before taking the cup. ‘Can I share some with the kitty? His bowl seems almost empty.’

The innkeeper nods approvingly and watches the kid carry the cup carefully to the corner and pour some of it into the Thing’s bowl.

‘Are you alone?’ he asks. ‘Where are your mother and father?’

The boy comes back to the bar, taking slow, careful steps not to spill the remaining milk.

‘Dad is over there,’ he waves to the town square window. There is, in fact, a sad, middle-aged man sitting there, on an almost empty terrace of the nearby beer garden. He doesn’t seem to have noticed that his son is no longer running around the square with a couple of neighbourhood kids.

‘Mom is in the hospital,’ the boy sighs. ‘Dad says she’s coming home soon, but I don’t think she is.’

The innkeeper nods sympathetically. He chooses a couple of dried flowers from the heap drying on the bar, puts them in a small paper bag, then adds something from a small wooden box he keeps on the top shelf.

‘Give this to your mom. Tell her to add it to her tea. I’m not sure it would help, not without seeing her or knowing what ails her – but there’s a chance.’

‘I knew you could make potions!’ the boy exclaims, but drops the topic under the innkeeper’s stern gaze. He puts the bag carefully into the pocket of his jacket and climbs down from the stool.

‘Thank you. I have to go now, or Dad will be worried.’

‘Of course,’ the innkeeper says, keeping relief from his voice. ‘See you around.’

He watches the boy run towards the terrace and tell his father something; he doesn’t hear the conversation but he can guess it all right. The boy would be telling stories of the cafe, of course – the ‘kitty’ and the bug wings and the tall man behind the bar who’s definitely making potions. The father would know, of course, like all locals do, that there was in fact an inn at that building – but that it’s been closed for almost a century, and since then the door was locked.

The innkeeper wonders idly how the boy managed to open it. Maybe nobody told him yet it can’t be opened, he thinks.
